\section{Introduction}

Weather affects how people use electricity. Cold weather increases demand for heating,
while hot weather causes air conditioners to run longer. Strong winds draw heat away
from structures more quickly, further increasing heating demand. Humidity changes how
warm a building feels, which shifts demand for both cooling and
heating~\cite{montel2024weather}. Electricity consumption also follows daily and weekly
patterns independent of weather, since usage tends to vary by hour of the day and by day
of the week~\cite{eia2011demand}.

The goal of this project is to predict how weather conditions in Finland, namely
temperature, wind, and humidity, along with the hour of day, weekday and month, affect
electricity consumption. This is done by combining data from Fingrid, the Finnish
electricity transmission system operator, and the Finnish Meteorological Institute (FMI).

Section~\ref{sec:problem} describes the data and formulates the task as a regression
problem. Section~\ref{sec:methods} explains how the data was prepared and the features
chosen, presents the three methods used (polynomial regression, a decision tree and a
random forest) together with their loss function, and describes how the data was split
for validation and testing. Section~\ref{sec:results} compares the training and validation
errors of the methods, chooses the final method and reports its test error.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} interprets the results, discusses the limitations of the study
and suggests directions for future work.

%==============================================================
\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

The data in this study comes from Fingrid and the Finnish Meteorological Institute (FMI).
The electricity data is the ``Electricity consumption in Finland'' dataset from Fingrid's open
data service~\cite{fingrid2026consumption}, comprising 119\,369 measurements, and the weather
data consists of 52\,589 hourly observations from the FMI Tapiola weather station, downloaded
from FMI's open data service~\cite{fmi2026observations}. Both cover the years 2020--2025.

Fingrid reported consumption hourly before 13 June 2023 and every 15 minutes thereafter,
so the Fingrid data was first aggregated to hourly resolution by averaging all measurements
within each hour. The two datasets were then merged on the hourly timestamp. 250 hours
were discarded because they lacked temperature, wind speed or humidity observations,
leaving a final dataset of 52\,241 data points.

Each data point represents one hour and has seven numerical features: air temperature
in degrees Celsius (\feat{temp_c}), wind speed in metres per second (\feat{wind_ms}), relative
humidity as a percentage (\feat{humid_pc}), a cyclic encoding of the hour of day as sine and
cosine (\feat{hour_sin}, \feat{hour_cos}), the weekday as an integer from 1 to 7 (\feat{weekday}),
and the month as an integer from 1 to 12 (\feat{month}). The hour of day is encoded cyclically
so that hour 23 and hour 0 are treated as adjacent rather than maximally distant. The label is
the average electricity consumption in Finland during that hour, measured in
megawatt-hours (\feat{consumption_mwh}). Since the label is continuous, this is a supervised
regression problem. The first rows of the merged data are shown in Table~\ref{tab:raw-data}.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Preparing the data}
\label{sec:data}

We selected temperature, wind, and humidity based on known physical effects on
heating and cooling demand, and included hour of day, weekday, and month to capture
daily and seasonal consumption patterns. The plots in Figures~\ref{fig:temp}--\ref{fig:humid}
and the correlation matrix in Figure~\ref{fig:corr} confirm that consumption varies with each
of these features: temperature has by far the strongest relationship with consumption,
consumption follows a clear daily and seasonal pattern and is lower at weekends, while wind
speed and humidity have weaker effects.

\subsection{Models and loss function}
\label{sec:models}

Below, a data point is written as $(\mathbf{x}, y)$, where
$\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_7)^T$ holds the features \feat{temp_c}, \feat{wind_ms},
\feat{humid_pc}, \feat{hour_sin}, \feat{hour_cos}, \feat{weekday} and \feat{month} in this
order, and $y$ is the measured consumption \feat{consumption_mwh}. The training set is
$\{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{m}$.

\paragraph{Loss function.}
Since our dataset consists of data points with numeric labels, this is a regression
problem, and we chose the squared error loss
\begin{equation}
  L\bigl((\mathbf{x}, y), h\bigr) = \bigl( y - h(\mathbf{x}) \bigr)^2,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $h(\mathbf{x})$ is the predicted consumption. Squared error loss penalizes large
prediction errors much more than small ones. This is useful for our problem because large
errors in predicted electricity consumption could cause grid operators to over- or
under-supply electricity, which is more costly than a small prediction error.

All three models were trained with squared error loss. The polynomial regression minimizes
it directly over the training data. The decision tree chooses each split to minimize the
squared error within the resulting groups of training points, and predicts the mean
consumption of the training points in each leaf. The random forest grows 100 such trees on
bootstrap samples with random subsets of features, and predicts the average of the trees'
outputs. Using the same loss for all models makes their errors directly comparable.

\paragraph{Polynomial regression.}
The first model is polynomial regression. Its hypothesis space consists of all polynomials
of degree at most $d$ in the seven features. For $d = 2$ this is
\begin{equation}
  h(\mathbf{x}) = w_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{7} w_j x_j + \sum_{j=1}^{7} \sum_{k=j}^{7} w_{jk}\, x_j x_k,
  \label{eq:poly}
\end{equation}
which has 36 weights: one intercept, 7 linear terms, 7 squared terms ($j = k$) and 21
pairwise products ($j < k$). The degree $d$ is a hyperparameter. We tried $d = 1$ to $4$
and chose the value with the lowest validation error (Section~\ref{sec:val-errors}). The
model is linear in its weights, so it is fitted as ordinary least squares on the expanded
features, i.e.\ the weights minimize the average loss~\eqref{eq:loss} on the training set,
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{w}} \; \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \bigl( y^{(i)} - h(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) \bigr)^2 .
  \label{eq:erm}
\end{equation}

We started from a polynomial model because the correlation matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:corr})
shows a strong linear relationship between temperature and consumption ($r = -0.77$), and
because it trains in seconds on about 35\,000 rows and has interpretable coefficients. A
purely linear predictor ($d = 1$) is not enough, though. The scatter plot in
Figure~\ref{fig:temp} shows that consumption stops falling above roughly
15\,\textdegree C, which a straight line cannot follow, so squared terms are needed. The
product terms let the effect of one feature depend on another: wind should raise heating
demand more on a cold day than on a mild one, and the daily cycle may look different in
January than in July.

\paragraph{Decision tree.}
The second model is a decision tree for regression. A tree splits the feature space into
rectangular regions $R_1, \dots, R_K$ by asking a sequence of threshold questions on single
features (for example $\feat{temp_c} \le 2.5$), and predicts the average training
consumption of the region a data point falls in:
\begin{equation}
  h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat{y}_k \, \mathbb{1}\{\mathbf{x} \in R_k\},
  \qquad
  \hat{y}_k = \frac{1}{|R_k|} \sum_{i:\, \mathbf{x}^{(i)} \in R_k} y^{(i)},
  \label{eq:tree}
\end{equation}
where $|R_k|$ is the number of training points in region $R_k$. The hypothesis space is
therefore the set of piecewise constant functions defined by such axis-aligned splits, with
the tree limited to a depth of at most $D$. We tried $D = 2$ to $20$ and chose the depth with
the lowest validation error, since a tree that is too deep memorises the training data.

We chose a tree because it makes no assumption about the shape of the relationship. It can
follow the flattening of the temperature curve, and it finds interactions between features
on its own, without us listing them as in the polynomial model. It also suits the weekday
and month features, which are stored as integers: a tree can separate weekends from working
days or winter from summer with one or two splits, whereas a polynomial has to treat those
integers as a numeric scale. Each split is chosen to minimize the squared error within the
resulting regions, so the tree is trained with the same loss as the polynomial regression.

\paragraph{Random forest.}
The third model is a random forest, which predicts the average of $B = 100$ regression
trees $h^{(1)}, \dots, h^{(B)}$ of the form~\eqref{eq:tree}:
\begin{equation}
  h(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} h^{(b)}(\mathbf{x}).
  \label{eq:forest}
\end{equation}
Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample of the training set, drawn with replacement, and
at every split only a random subset of $m_{\text{feat}}$ features is considered. The
hypothesis space is thus the set of averages of 100 such trees. These are still piecewise
constant functions, but with far finer steps than a single tree. The trees are not
depth-limited; instead each leaf must contain at least $n_{\min}$ training points. We tried
$m_{\text{feat}} = 1$ to $7$ and $n_{\min} \in \{1, 10\}$ and chose the combination with the
lowest validation error, keeping the number of trees fixed at 100.

A single tree is unstable: a small change in the training data can change its splits and
predictions noticeably. Averaging many trees that have each seen slightly different data and
features reduces this variance while keeping the tree's ability to model non-linear effects
and interactions. The random forest is the most flexible of our three models, so comparing
it with the polynomial regression shows whether that extra flexibility is useful for this
problem. The individual trees are trained with squared error, as above.

\subsection{Model validation}
\label{sec:validation}

For model validation the dataset was split chronologically by calendar year. The years
2020--2023 were used for training (34\,717 data points, 66.5\,\%), 2024 for validation
(8\,778 data points, 16.8\,\%) and 2025 for testing (8\,746 data points, 16.7\,\%). The
test set was set aside and used only once, to evaluate the final chosen model.

We chose a chronological split instead of a random one for two reasons. First, the model is
meant to predict future consumption, so the validation and test sets must come from periods
it has not seen during training. Second, consumption in consecutive hours is highly
correlated, so a random split would place nearly identical data points in both the training
and validation sets and give an over-optimistic error estimate.

Splitting on whole calendar years also ensures that each set covers a complete annual cycle.
Had the split been made at an arbitrary point in time, the test set might have contained only
summer or only winter months, and the reported error would have reflected a single season
rather than year-round performance.

Errors on a set of $n$ data points with predictions $\hat{y}^{(i)} = h(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})$ are
reported as the root mean squared error, the mean absolute percentage error and the
coefficient of determination:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)}\bigr)^2},
  \qquad
  \text{MAPE} = \frac{100\,\%}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\bigl|y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)}\bigr|}{y^{(i)}},
  \\[0.5em]
  R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)}\bigr)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y^{(i)} - \bar{y}\bigr)^2},
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{y}$ is the mean consumption of the evaluated set. RMSE is the square root of the
average squared error loss~\eqref{eq:loss} and is in MWh, MAPE is the average absolute error
as a percentage of actual consumption, and $R^2$ is the share of the variance in consumption
explained by the model, relative to the variance of the evaluated set. Only the squared error
loss is used for training, and validation RMSE was used for choosing hyperparameters and the
final model.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Training and validation errors}
\label{sec:val-errors}

Each model's hyperparameters were chosen by validation error (Section~\ref{sec:models}). For
the polynomial regression the validation RMSE was 777, 543, 561 and 563\,MWh for degrees 1 to
4, so degree~2 was chosen. The best settings for the other models were maximum depth $D = 9$
for the decision tree, and $m_{\text{feat}} = 4$ with $n_{\min} = 10$ for the random forest.
Table~\ref{tab:results} shows the resulting errors.

The decision tree has the highest validation error. Its training error is low, but it does
not carry over to new data, which is typical for a single deep tree. The random forest has
the lowest training error and the largest gap between training and validation error, so it
fits the training data more closely than the others, yet its validation error is still
higher than that of the polynomial regression.

\subsection{Final chosen method}

The final chosen method is polynomial regression of degree 2, since it has the lowest
validation error on every measure (RMSE 543\,MWh, MAPE 4.36\,\% and $R^2 = 0.900$, compared
with 568\,MWh, 4.42\,\% and 0.890 for the random forest). It is also the simplest of the
three models, with 36 weights, and its training and validation errors are close to each
other (515 vs.\ 543\,MWh), which suggests it does not overfit.

\subsection{Test error of the final method}

The chosen model was evaluated once on the test set (the year 2025, 8\,746 data points). The
test set was not used for training or for choosing any setting. The test errors were
RMSE = 804\,MWh, MAPE = 6.81\,\% and $R^2 = 0.669$, clearly worse than on the validation set.

Most of this increase comes from a systematic offset. On average the model underestimates
2025 consumption by 625\,MWh, about 6.5\,\% of the mean test-year consumption of
9\,652\,MWh; the mean over the training years was 9\,172\,MWh. With this constant offset
removed, the test RMSE is 505\,MWh, about the same as the training error (515\,MWh). The
model therefore captures how consumption varies with weather and time of day, but it cannot
predict a change in the overall consumption level between years, because none of the
features describes such long-term trends. $R^2$ falls further because it is relative to the
variance of the evaluated set (Section~\ref{sec:validation}), and consumption varied less in
2025 (standard deviation 1\,396\,MWh vs.\ 1\,714\,MWh in 2024), so the same error is a larger
share of the total variation. For comparison, the random forest reached a slightly lower test
RMSE (768\,MWh), but this was not used in choosing the model.

%==============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study compared polynomial regression (degree 2), a decision tree and a random forest for
predicting Finland's hourly electricity consumption from weather and time features. All three
explained most of the variation on the 2024 validation data ($R^2$ 0.870--0.900).
Temperature was by far the strongest predictor ($r = -0.77$), while wind and humidity were
much weaker. Polynomial regression of degree 2 was chosen because it had the lowest
validation error on every measure (RMSE 543\,MWh, MAPE 4.36\,\%, $R^2 = 0.900$). The
tree-based models fit the training data better (random forest training RMSE 351\,MWh) but
generalised worse, which suggests the relationship is mostly smooth and their extra
flexibility brought little benefit.

On the 2025 test set the chosen model reached RMSE = 804\,MWh, clearly worse than on
validation. Most of the increase was a systematic underestimate of 625\,MWh. Without it the
test RMSE would be 505\,MWh, in line with the training error. The model therefore captures
how consumption varies with weather and time, but not changes in the overall consumption
level between years. The random forest's slightly lower test RMSE (768\,MWh) did not
influence the choice, which was made on validation alone.

The results are therefore not optimal, and the study has several limitations. Only one year
each was used for validation and testing, so the estimates reflect the weather and demand of
those years. No feature describes long-term changes such as electrification, heat pumps or
industrial changes. Weather data comes from a single station while consumption is national,
and public holidays are not included. The polynomial can also give unrealistic predictions
outside the training range, and a random forest cannot extrapolate at all.

Future work could use rolling-origin validation, training on all past years and testing on
the following one, to get error estimates that do not depend on a single year. The features
could be extended with weather from several stations and with public holidays, and the model
could be given information about the overall consumption level, for example the previous
year's average consumption as a feature, or be retrained on the most recent data.
